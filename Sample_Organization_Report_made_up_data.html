  2. 9
    Long-retained EBS snapshots still in Standard storage (older than 365 days) about $1K a year
    How it was estimated

    365 days is the longest default retention target, a default assumption, not an AWS limit. The figure assumes the old snapshots of a volume hold at most one full copy of it (the volume size, or less when the snapshots are smaller), at the standard rate ($0.05 per GiB-month). Snapshots are incremental and share blocks, so what only the old ones hold is usually less; a volume whose data changes heavily can hold more. Age alone does not make a snapshot waste. Archiving is not priced as a saving: an archived snapshot is a full copy, billed for at least 90 days, and takes up to 72 hours to restore. It is an estimate for the owner to check, not a saving.

Built into the service What these databases have now: automated backups, which restore to any second in the retention window (at most 35 days). They stay in the account. A manual snapshot copied to another account is the service's own way to keep a copy outside it.

Beyond AWS-native tools Isolated copies of RDS and Aurora held outside the account, with an archive tier for retention beyond the 35-day ceiling of automated backups. Archived backups can be searched for rows after a wait of up to 48 hours; a restore is of the whole instance or cluster.

DynamoDB

Built into the service What these tables have now: point-in-time recovery, which restores to any second in its window (at most 35 days) into a new table. It stays in the account.

AWS Backup With advanced DynamoDB backup on, copies to another account or a logically air-gapped vault, and a cold tier.

Beyond AWS-native tools Backup through DynamoDB Streams kept in an isolated account, with a search of a backup by key and a download of the matching records, and a rollback of selected partitions in place (AWS restores of a whole table create a new table). Its recovery point is about 15 minutes, where point-in-time recovery is to the second within its window.

S3

Built into the service What these buckets have now: versioning or replication, which bring back an overwritten or deleted object. Both sit where the account's own credentials can delete them. Replication to another account with S3 Object Lock keeps a copy those credentials cannot.

AWS Backup S3 backup (it needs versioning on) to a vault in another account or a logically air-gapped vault.

Beyond AWS-native tools Backup scoped to the buckets, prefixes or objects that matter, kept in an isolated account, restorable as one key, a prefix or many buckets to another bucket, Region or account, with bulk rollback of a bucket to a point in time. Purpose

How this review works

Why this review exists

Which resources matter most to the business, what each could recover from, and where protection may fall short or cost more than it needs to.

AWS gives you many ways to protect data: automated backups, point-in-time recovery, snapshots, versioning, backup plans, vault locking, air-gapped vaults, cross-Region copies. Each is reasonable on its own. Together they make one question hard to answer from any console: across every account, what is protected, how well, and at what cost?

Drift is normal when many teams share an estate. Accounts accumulate, teams set protection for their own workloads, and sandboxes become production. None of this reflects poorly on anyone; usually no one has had the whole picture at once.

This review is that picture: your own metadata, read back in one place and organized around recovery. It is not a score. Where a finding differs from what you intended, that difference is the useful part.

The report names individual resources only where they are the reason for a finding. All 258 are in the CSV files for the teams doing the work.

What this review can and cannot see

This review reads AWS Backup (plans, vaults, recovery points, jobs and restore tests, for every resource type it supports) and AWS-native protection: RDS and Aurora automated backups and snapshots, DynamoDB point-in-time recovery and backups, EBS snapshots and lifecycle policies, and S3 versioning and replication. It cannot see other backup tools, because the read-only role has no access to their records. A resource protected only by one shows here as having no recovery copy, unless that tool leaves a snapshot AWS can show. The overrides file can record it as protected elsewhere, with an owner and a review date.
